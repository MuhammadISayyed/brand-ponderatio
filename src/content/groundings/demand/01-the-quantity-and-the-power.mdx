Ask an economist what demand is and you will be shown a schedule: a list of
quantities that would be bought at each price, or a curve drawn through them.
Ask what the schedule is built from and you will be shown transactions —
things people actually did, at prices that actually obtained. The schedule is
a record. It is an honest one, and it is not the thing we were asking about.

The gap shows up in ordinary usage. When an analyst says that demand for a
good *fell*, they are not merely reporting that fewer units changed hands.
Fewer units change hands for many reasons: the shelves were empty, the
delivery was late, the shop shut at four. We distinguish these without
effort. A supply failure is not a fall in demand, though both leave the same
entry in the ledger.<Sidenote>Which is already enough to show that demand is
not identical to quantity transacted. Two situations agreeing in every
transaction can disagree about demand.</Sidenote> So the word is tracking
something other than the count — something that was there, unchanged, while
the delivery was late.

## What the schedule presupposes

<Cite source="robbins">Robbins</Cite> defined economics as the study of
behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means with alternative
uses, and the definition was a narrowing on purpose: it moved the subject
away from the substance of what people want and toward the form of choosing
under constraint. <Cite source="samuelson">Samuelson</Cite> completed the move
a few years later. If preference is to be respectable it should be *revealed*
— read off from choices rather than posited behind them. What you would buy is
inferred from what you did buy.

There is a discipline in that, and it bought economics a great deal. It also
quietly changed the subject. A revealed preference is a summary of a history.
It is defined over choices that were made, in conditions that obtained, at
prices that were posted. It says nothing whatever about the case that did not
arise — and the case that did not arise is precisely what a demand schedule
claims to describe. The schedule asserts what *would* be bought at prices
never posted, from a record containing only prices that were.

So the schedule needs something the record cannot supply. It needs the buyer
to have had, at the time, a standing readiness to buy at those other prices —
a readiness that was there whether or not the price ever fell far enough to
find out. The curve is a claim about unexercised readiness, drawn from a
ledger of exercised ones.

That standing readiness is what this grounding is about, and it has a name in
metaphysics. The name is not *quantity*. It is a **disposition**: a property
whose nature is to be directed at a manifestation that may never occur.
Fragility is the schoolbook case. A glass is fragile now, sitting undisturbed
on a shelf, and would have been fragile if it had never been struck and had
eventually been melted down. Its fragility is not the breaking. It is what
would be exercised in the breaking.

## Tendencies were the original vocabulary

This is closer to a repatriation than an import. <Cite source="mill">Mill</Cite>
held that the laws of political economy are laws of *tendency* — statements
about how a cause operates when it operates alone, which stay true when the
tendency is overwhelmed by others and no corresponding regularity appears in
the data at all. A tendency that never shows itself is still a tendency. On
Mill's account this is not an embarrassment to be explained away but the
normal condition of any science whose objects are not sealed in a
laboratory.<Sidenote>His example is that a body's tendency to fall is not
falsified by holding it up. The tendency and the resultant motion are
different things, and only the second is observed.</Sidenote>

<Cite source="cartwright">Cartwright</Cite> makes the modern version of the
argument, and makes it about measurement. Her claim is that the sciences which
work do not traffic in regularities but in *capacities* — stable causal powers
that can be carried from one situation to another, which is exactly what a
regularity cannot do. A regularity holds where it holds. A capacity travels,
because it is a fact about the thing rather than about the circumstances the
thing was in. And the reason econometrics is so hard, on her account, is that
it keeps trying to read capacities off regularities produced by many
capacities acting at once.

If that is right then the demand curve is not a law and never was one. It is a
trace: the visible residue left where a capacity has been exercised under one
particular arrangement of everything else. Change the arrangement and the
trace changes while the capacity does not.

## What the rest of this has to establish

Naming demand a disposition is easy, and by itself nearly empty. Every
interesting question is downstream, and two are hard enough to threaten the
project.

The first is whether the move is real or merely verbal. There is a standing
tradition on which dispositional talk is a promissory note — a way of
gesturing at a conditional not yet spelled out. If *is fragile* just means
*would break if struck*, then *demands at price p* just means *would buy at
price p*, and nothing has been gained: we have relabelled the counterfactual
the schedule was already making. Part II argues that this reduction fails, and
fails for reasons that transfer intact to the economic case.

The second is composition. Whatever demand is, economics needs to add it up:
individual demands into market demand, market demands into aggregate demand.
Quantities add. Powers do not — they compose, which is a different operation
with different results, and the difference is not a technicality. Part III
argues that much of what is puzzling about aggregation is an artifact of
having assumed the wrong arithmetic.
