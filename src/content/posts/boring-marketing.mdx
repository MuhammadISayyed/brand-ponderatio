Marketing is multidisciplinary, even in the narrowest of definitions. Think of
the narrowest definition of marketing you've come across. What is it? For me
it's that marketing is just about ads. But even ads, when done well, are
complex and have so many disciplines baked into them.

This complex nature is rooted in the fact that marketing is about providing
value for the customer and communicating it effectively. For this to happen, so
many things have to be considered. This makes marketing hard to explain, let
alone master.

In this essay I argue that when marketing complexity is attacked instead of
cherished, marketing becomes stale to the point of failing to stimulate any
kind of value creation. I also argue that this attack is the result of
different sources of pressure that, collectively, have led to the
aforementioned outcome.

## Pressure from the top

Sometimes you zoom in too much trying to see things clearly, yet as a
by-product of that, your perception gets distorted and the thing you're looking
at loses its shape. This is how many boardrooms treat marketing. Marketing is
part operational, part strategic. It's part short-term and part long-term. Part
about what happens internally, part about what happens outside.

Being all over the place isn't easy to grasp. So limiting the areas in which
marketing can have an effect is a good first step to easily understand it. But
even then, marketing is notoriously difficult to measure. So limiting its
contribution isn't enough. You've got to focus on the things that are easily
measurable. This is a good second step. Knowing that they should only stick to
what's clearly seen and understood, a marketer has very little motivation to
connect the dots. Seeking to understand the underlying context of day-to-day
operations becomes an invisible effort that's neither expected nor rewarded.
Today, we see CMOs talk about the tangibles more than anything else. And we see
the role of the CMO already disappearing. This is mysterious. If marketers are
being obedient, why are they severed? The short answer is that in becoming
obedient, we've become useless.

Marketing isn't the kind of job in which you're expected to simply go to work,
perform a number of chores and go back home. Even if C-levels tell you
otherwise. When numbers (the ones that really matter) go south, you're a good
candidate for blame and punishment. We're disconnected from our customers, they
say. You're not doing your job well.

## Pressure from within

Working in such a field pushes people to employ all kinds of defense mechanisms
to survive. Some find solace in trends, capitalizing on the fugitive rush of
adrenaline that hits management when any innovation announces itself, promising
huge gains in efficiency, effectiveness or capital. Others find great success in
calling marketing something else altogether. In this, they're more costume and
makeup artists than marketers. This has been repeating forever now. Growth
hacking is a good example. In 2010, [Sean
Ellis](https://www.startup-marketing.com/where-are-all-the-growth-hackers/) told
us that a growth hacker is:

> [...] a person whose true north is growth. Everything they do is scrutinized
> by its potential impact on scalable growth.

At the time growth felt prestigious. It was where the technicians of SEO,
performance marketing and the like went as a reward for their technical
prowess. It felt like growth hackers had superpowers. Ellis' definition is all
about being growth-oriented methodically. That's good and all, but I struggle
to find it meaningfully different from the regular responsibilities of a
marketer. What is growth other than exchanging value with customers at the
highest rate possible? The more customers you exchange value with, the bigger
you grow, right? So a growth hacker does this faster than others? Who are the
others? Maybe marketers?

The growth community kept optimizing for the "methodical" bit of methodical
growth to the level that it became an objective that overtook growth itself.
You see, growth is also contextual. With time, context became secondary to
experimentation. Despite focusing on acquiring customers at the highest
possible velocity (or maybe because of it), customers fled. Why? Because the
mere act of acquiring a new customer became a goal in and of
itself.<Sidenote>Goodhart's law states that when a measure becomes a target, it
ceases to be a good measure. This is exactly what growth people did with
acquisition, rendering it a useless measure of their own efforts.</Sidenote>
Growth people developed a more mature version of growth hacking and added
retention to the equation to balance the imbalance caused by targeting
acquisition alone. This way the job of growth hackers became creating and
keeping a customer.<Sidenote>This is <Cite source="drucker">Peter
Drucker</Cite>'s definition of the purpose of business. This way growth went a
full cycle, from being something very specific that's outside the scope of
marketing to being synonymous with how business functions.</Sidenote>

Things are similar with product marketing too. I don't know where this came
from, but it seems that product *marketers* feel like [they're not
marketers](https://thecompetenetwork.com/were-not-marketers), claiming that
their role is much more than that. It's about understanding the customer,
collaborating with product and sales teams, and doing cool stuff like
positioning, messaging and maybe a bit of copywriting. I don't know about you,
but to me this seems an awful lot like what a marketer would work on.

The repackaging of marketing can be seen as a defense mechanism practiced by
people who want to save their careers from falling into the trenches of being
seen as a failing ad person, people who see that vanilla marketing isn't
respected anymore. It needs a bit of spice and glamour to it. In a career that
lacks a ladder, people will lump, jump and hop for a living. And the last thing
marketers may care about is losing the joy of figuring things out.

## Dogmatic pressures

You can imagine that when working amidst all of this chaos, it's grounding to
find authoritative figures who preach universal marketing laws that apply
across different markets and industries. The data is solid, the research has
depth to it, and on this front, it's an incredible tool in the marketer's
toolkit. Problems arise when the people who provide the research try to make it
*the* toolkit instead of being part of it. It's as if you were having a heart
attack and someone came and resuscitated you and put you on a ventilator. They
then believe that being on a ventilator is the *only* way to survive a heart
attack. They write a book and name it *How People Grow*, in which they argue
that in their datasets, people who live long enough to have a heart attack are
always put on a ventilator.

It's incredibly helpful to have rigorous minds providing us with different
descriptions of how markets behave and brands grow. But it's ridiculously
limiting to impose descriptions as laws. In something as context- and
path-dependent as marketing, it's astonishing that people believe that we're
limited by a handful of laws that constrain businesses, markets and behaviors.
This contributes to the staleness of marketing. Marketers may believe that
everything is answered, and the many business configurations that actually work
and thrive are ignored for the sake of law universality.

## The verdict

The product of this multi-level pressure is boring marketing. It's marketing
that doesn't try to answer some questions because they already are answered,
and doesn't ask other ones because they're not compensated for doing so. It's
marketing that's not interested in understanding context or in being proactive;
instead, it creates marketers who try to watch their backs.

## In praise of demanding marketing

Marketing, as this essay argues, is incredibly demanding. A marketer's first
responsibility is to continuously make sense of the context and calibrate the
company's actions accordingly. This means that a marketer is constantly
fighting, not only their prejudices, but also others' shallow framings of the
complex, path-dependent challenges they face day to day.

Marketing demands the marketer to juggle zooming in on daily operations,
zooming out to see the elements of the system in which they operate, and
maneuvering between pressures from boardrooms, colleagues, and whatever is
debated today amongst marketers. Between all of that, the marketer is compelled
to settle down on whatever these dynamics seem to favor instead of trying to
find their own path. Can you blame them?

But for those who choose to keep the joy of discovery, understanding and
coherence in marketing, those who decide to respect the complexity of marketing
instead of reducing it, those who aren't comfortable with easy answers that
aren't tied to clear commercial excellence... they know the challenge is the
reward and have come to terms with all of it.
